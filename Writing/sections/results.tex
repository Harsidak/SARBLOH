\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Our empirical evaluation demonstrates that action budget exhaustion on ARC-AGI-3 is driven predominantly by 
perceptual misinduction rather than downstream planning failure. While deterministically computed geometric relations 
generalize across environments unconditionally, high-level affordance roles remain latent and require interactive 
disambiguation through active execution probes. To establish an uncontaminated assessment of out-of-distribution reasoning, 
we evaluate \textsc{Sarbloh Agent} primarily on the official held-out Kaggle benchmark, supplemented by hardware-matched 
comparative baselines and continuous systems telemetry on public environments to isolate architectural contributions from compute scale.

\subsection{Hidden-Set Evaluation Trajectory}
\label{sec:trajectory}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/leaderboard.pdf}
\caption{Evaluation trajectory across completed submissions on the official held-out Kaggle ARC-AGI-3 benchmark. The solid curve tracks cumulative peak efficiency score, hollow markers indicate regressive exploratory trials, and annotations denote primary architectural interventions.}
\label{fig:leaderboard}
\end{figure*}

Because the official evaluation suite executes strictly offline on undisclosed game environments, it provides an uncontaminated benchmark of generalization (Figure~\ref{fig:leaderboard}). Initial baselines adapting the Prime Agent framework with open-weight models achieved scores between 0.00 and 0.27. Diagnostic trace inspection across ten games revealed that the model engaged in unguided search, initiating over 350 voluntary level restarts without resolving a single goal state. 

Successive architectural interventions systematically advanced performance across five distinct milestones:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Serving and Backbone Upgrade (Score 5.14)}---Migrating the serving runtime to Qwen3.8-Flash-Next on SGLang with speculative decoding, prefix-cached prompt structuring, and ten concurrent game slots established our initial operational baseline.
    \item \textbf{Unified Programmatic Execution (Score 5.65)}---Merging action dispatch directly into the Python kernel via \code{await play()} eliminated disruptive tool-switching loops between planning and actuation (Section~\ref{sec:acting}).
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Context Drainage (Score 6.06)}---Replacing lossy model-generated summaries with deterministic context truncation prevented the accumulation of hallucinated state histories (Section~\ref{sec:context}).
    \item \textbf{Symbolic Memory and AST Tracking (Score 10.98)}---Persistent level-keyed memory dictionaries, real-time code graph tracking via AST inspection, and state revisit guardrails nearly doubled the evaluation score.
    \item \textbf{Integrated Multimodal Perception (Score 16.53)}---Re-enabling the full deterministic perception engine alongside refined turn-based context drainage established our peak efficiency score, representing a 13.7$\times$ improvement over the Milestone~\#1 baseline.
\end{enumerate}

Three exploratory submissions exhibited temporary score regressions (0.35, 2.97, and 1.96, with the latter exceeding the nine-hour execution budget), which we document to illustrate the boundary conditions of runtime stability. Because the final milestone simultaneously integrated refined context drainage and the reactivation of the perception engine following intermediate ablation trials, the perception engine's exact isolated share of the final 51\% gain remains coupled with these complementary runtime optimizations.

\subsection{Hardware-Controlled Comparative Evaluation}
\label{sec:matched}

To isolate architectural contributions from compute hardware, we evaluated \textsc{Sarbloh} directly against the Milestone~\#1 winning Duck harness \citep{duck2026} on identical server hardware (one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000) and identical Qwen3.8-Flash-Next model weights served via SGLang. The baseline deployed four concurrent execution passes per game, whereas \textsc{Sarbloh} executed a single pass across the 25 public games managed by our utility-driven priority scheduler.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:curves}, \textsc{Sarbloh Agent} consistently accelerates solution discovery throughout the execution window, clearing 73 levels versus 54 at one hour, 97 versus 63 at two hours, and 136 versus 103 at four hours. Across the complete evaluation, \textsc{Sarbloh} resolves 143 of 183 total levels in 8.2 hours, exceeding the 129 distinct levels completed across all four baseline passes combined. This performance advantage stems directly from dynamic priority scheduling, which concentrates GPU bandwidth on game states exhibiting high marginal progress potential rather than partitioning compute uniformly across redundant parallel trajectories.

\paragraph{Comparison with Frontier Model Baselines.}
Figure~\ref{fig:pergame} compares the per-game efficiency scores of \textsc{Sarbloh} against five frontier models evaluated on the 25 public environments. When aggregating the best performance achieved across evaluation trials to establish an empirical upper envelope, \textsc{Sarbloh} achieves a mean efficiency score of 72.5, leading frontier baselines on 21 of the 25 games and achieving complete resolution on 13. Crucially, even under a single-pass execution regime on our single-GPU budget, \textsc{Sarbloh} achieves a mean score of 59.4, outperforming the strongest frontier model baseline of 54.8. The architecture demonstrates substantial performance advantages on complex environments such as \code{g50t}, scoring 54.6 while all evaluated frontier models fail to surpass 0.28. Conversely, \textsc{Sarbloh} trails GPT-6.1 Sol on four environments, including \code{dc22} (39.6 versus 47.6), \code{wa30} (26.3 versus 76.6), and most notably \code{sp80} (22.4 versus 60.1) and \code{tn36} (7.7 versus 75.0), where unconstrained API inference budgets favor broad exploratory search.
\subsection{Where the Points Are Lost}
\label{sec:efficiency}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/score_budget.pdf}
\caption{The 23~October run (143 levels, mean 59.4). (a)~Agent-to-human action ratio on each cleared level; hatched bars are at or below the human count. (b)~The 40.6 points lost from a perfect score, computed with the official scorer's level weights: levels after the first uncleared one, the first uncleared level of each game, and the action overhead on cleared levels, net of the 115\% cap.}
\label{fig:scorebudget}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Once a level is understood, the agent plays it in fewer actions than the human baseline.}
The median ratio of agent to human actions on the 143 cleared levels was 0.77, and 66\% of cleared levels took no more actions than the human (Figure~\ref{fig:scorebudget}a). Under Equation~\eqref{eq:rhae}, 62\% of cleared levels earned the full 115\% cap. The agent plans a level in code and sends the whole path in one call; once it knows what it is looking at, acting is not where it loses.

\paragraph{Levels the agent never understood cost more than twice as much as slow play on levels it did.}
We split the 40.6 points lost from a perfect score exactly, using the official scorer's weights (Figure~\ref{fig:scorebudget}b). Levels never reached, because the game stalled on an earlier one, cost 20.1 points; the first uncleared level of each game cost 9.2; action overhead on cleared levels cost 11.3. So 29.3 of the 40.6 points, 72\%, are on levels the agent did not clear. We attribute most of these to perceptual misinduction, for two reasons visible in the traces: (i)~on stuck levels the agent rewrote its stated goal a median of 8 times, against once on cleared levels; and (ii)~it formed false beliefs about the game and acted on them, declaring \code{vc33} ``unsolvable as generated'' after 739 actions and then spending 1{,}240 more, and calling the layouts of \code{bp35} random. The same six games (\code{bp35}, \code{sp80}, \code{tn36}, \code{vc33}, \code{s5i5}, \code{lf52}) blocked every later level. These traces are from the briefing-off run, so they show the failure the engine is meant to remove, not the engine removing it.

\subsection{Failure Modes}
\label{sec:failures}

\paragraph{A low-budget prompt made four games idle for hours at their action cap.}
In the 23~October run, four games (\code{lf52}, \code{s5i5}, \code{vc33}, \code{re86}) stopped 2 to 15 actions short of the 2{,}000-action cap. The harness kept asking them to continue, and the model answered ``\code{Holding...}'' about 14{,}000 times, which was 1.24\,B of the run's 1.78\,B prompt tokens; the last level was cleared at 7.0\,h and the stalls kept the run alive to 8.2\,h. No game was waiting for the GPU during the stalls, so they cost wall clock, not levels. A cap of 400 turns per game now ends such games.

\paragraph{A placeholder in the history collapsed the agent.}
The two history-stubbing runs of Section~\ref{sec:rejected} are the starkest failure we saw: with its past code replaced by a comment, the model wrote the comment as its next cell in 97.7\% of calls and cleared 0 of 97 levels. Small open-weight models copy what sits in their context, and every token the harness places there is effectively an example.
